### Read in full

**Chalmers (2024), "Taking the simulation hypothesis seriously", *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research*, DOI 10.1111/phpr.13122** (reply to commentators Godfrey-Smith, Schneider, Schwitzgebel; author's PDF). Relevant content: the simulation hypothesis cannot be ruled out and is not a sceptical hypothesis when the simulation is perfect, since most of our beliefs would be true. Per Chalmers's summary, Schneider and Schwitzgebel argue that *other, imperfect* versions may be sceptical; Chalmers himself writes that "a low-grade model would be pretty easily detectable" (in the context of how much simulation is needed for rich social interaction). (A search-tool summary also says he states that his arguments do not depend on simulations being undetectable; I did not find that sentence in the extracted text, so it is not relied on.) No statistical treatment of detectability appears in the passages read; I searched the extracted text for "detect", "glitch", "evidence", "imperfect", "indistinguish", "testab" and "empirical" and read all hits.

**Chalmers (2024), "The Simulation Hypothesis: Metaphysics, Epistemology, Value", in *Oxford Studies in Philosophy of Mind* vol. 4, DOI 10.1093/9780198924159.003.0017 (author's PDF).** Three theses: if we are in a simulation the surrounding objects are real; we cannot know we are not in a simulation; we can live a good life in one. Epistemology: replying to Peacocke, he rejects the use of external perceptual evidence to rule out simulation (the "Sims won't have our evidence" appendix: with Weatherson 2003, externalists about evidence say a sim lacks our evidence; Chalmers argues that when most beings with experiences like ours are sims, this evidence is defeated, using zebra/hologram and 50%-cat analogies). The argument is by indifference over beings with matching experiences, not by statistical testing.

### Overlap with the present project

### Assessment

The qualitative content of theses T1 and T2 is **largely anticipated** by Chalmers's perfect/imperfect distinction and by the detection proposals in physics. The residue that may be new is (i) the explicit decomposition into three inferences with stated premises, (ii) quantitative bounds tied to a class of interventions (Props. 3–6), and (iii) the learned-world-model instantiation with the observer's own theory as part of the test. Whether any of these is new in the philosophical literature has not been established: the search was unsystematic, and several directly relevant sources (Brogioli, the glitch paper, Edge, Kelly's definitions, the Glymour–Malament–Manchak–Norton papers) were not read.
