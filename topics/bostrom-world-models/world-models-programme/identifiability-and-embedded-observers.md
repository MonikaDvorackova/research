# Identifiability and embedded observers: a formal framework

Purpose: state the central question precisely, define the notions the programme used loosely, and sort what is **established** from what is open. Nothing here is claimed as a new theorem: every proposition is marked K (known, with source), E (elementary, immediate from definitions) or C (conjecture/open). Source access levels are in `literature-and-novelty-map.md`.

## 1. Is the question well-defined?
*"Under what conditions can an intelligent observer embedded within a generative system identify the rules or properties of the system responsible for its observable reality?"* As stated it is **not** well-defined: "rules", "properties", "identify", "embedded" and "observer" are each underspecified, and the answer ranges from trivially yes to trivially no depending on them. It becomes well-posed once five things are fixed: (i) the class 𝓜 of candidate mechanisms and the *kind of property* asked about; (ii) the **interface** I (what the observer can do and see, including whether it can reset, replay, and reach all states); (iii) the **success notion** (exact identification in the limit; finite-sample error; computable within a resource budget); (iv) the **resources** R; (v) the **assumptions** that restrict 𝓜. The original motivation is preserved by treating "embedded" as a constraint on (ii) and (iv): one trajectory, no reset, causal restrictions on what can be probed, resources no greater than those of the system hosting the observer.

## 2. Objects
* **Mechanism** M: a controlled stochastic dynamical system with hidden state, M = (S, b₀, T, Ω): S state space, b₀ initial law, T(s′|s,a) transition, Ω(o|s,a) observation kernel, actions a ∈ A, observations o ∈ O. (POMDP / controlled HMM; deterministic and continuous-time systems are special cases.)
* **Interface** I = (A_I ⊆ A, Π_I, reset structure ρ, reach restriction 𝒞). Π_I is the class of admissible (possibly randomised, adaptive) policies; ρ ∈ {single trajectory, episodic with reset, replay with counterfactual state reset}; 𝒞 limits which parts of S can be affected (e.g. a causal patch).
* **Observation history** D = (a₁, o₁, …, a_n, o_n); under policy π ∈ Π_I mechanism M induces a law P_M^π on histories. Because policy factors cancel in the likelihood, P_M^π is determined by the **system-dynamics function** D_M(o | h, a) = P_M(o_{t} = o | history h, action a) on histories h reachable under Π_I (K: Singh–James–Rudary 2004 define the same object as the system-dynamics matrix; its rank is the minimal linear predictive-state dimension).
* **Hypothesis class** H, **observer policy** π, **resource budget** R (time, memory, number of interventions), **representations** (equations, programs, graphs) used to *describe* mechanisms.

## 3. Equivalence relations
* **D1 (interface-relative behaviour).** β_M^I := (P_M^π)_{π∈Π_I}.
* **D2 (observational equivalence relative to Π₀).** M ≈_{Π₀} M′ iff P_M^π = P_{M′}^π for all π ∈ Π₀. *Passive* observational equivalence: Π₀ = non-intervening policies. **Interventional equivalence** ≈_I: Π₀ = Π_I.
* **P1 (E).** Π₀ ⊆ Π₁ ⇒ ≈_{Π₁} ⊆ ≈_{Π₀}: more interventions give a finer partition. Strictness is mechanism-dependent (K: for DAGs, Hauser & Bühlmann 2012 prove interventional Markov equivalence classes are finer than observational ones; Bareinboim et al.'s Causal Hierarchy Theorem shows the three layers of Pearl's hierarchy almost never collapse).
* **D3 (reachable set).** R_I(M) = histories with positive probability under some π ∈ Π_I.
* **P2 (E; K in spirit: trace/bisimulation equivalence).** If M and M′ have equal dynamics on R_I(M) = R_I(M′) then M ≈_I M′. In particular mechanisms differing only on state–action pairs never reached under Π_I are I-equivalent, whatever the sample size. (This is the programme's Prop 1′, S1 `unreach`, T3a.)
* **Consequence.** The *identifiable content* of a world, for an observer with interface I, is the class [M]_I, not M. Everything below is a statement about [M]_I or about properties constant on it.

## 4. Identifiability notions
* **D4 (structural identifiability, parametrised).** For a parametrisation θ ↦ M_θ, θ is globally (locally) structurally identifiable iff β^I(θ′) = β^I(θ) ⇒ θ′ = θ (θ′ near θ). K: Rothenberg 1971, Bellman–Åström 1970 (not accessed). **Parametrisation-relative**: it is a property of the *representation*, not of the world. The invariant notion is identifiability of the class [M]_I.
* **D5 (identifiability of a property).** A property φ : 𝓜 → V is *I-identifiable* only if φ is constant on ≈_I classes (**P3, E**). Constancy is necessary, not sufficient: reliable identification *in the limit* additionally requires a topological condition on the problem — K: a hypothesis space is reliably identifiable iff it is a countable disjunction of refutable hypotheses (Kelly 1996, as reported in the SEP entry "Formal Learning Theory" §3.3; accessed via the SEP only).
* **D6 (statistical identifiability, finite noisy data).** Fix a budget (n episodes, noise σ, policy class). Two classes are statistically separable at level (α, β) iff some procedure has type-I ≤ α and power ≥ 1 − β. K: Le Cam's two-point method gives the lower bound ½(1 − TV(P₀ⁿ, P₁ⁿ)) on the sum-of-errors-type risk (Tsybakov 2009 §2; not accessed); under sequential testing T1 of phase 10 (Wald-type KL data-processing bound). **P4 (E from D6):** non-identifiability can be *practical*: ≈_I-distinct mechanisms with TV below a threshold for every admissible policy at budget n are statistically indistinguishable at that budget.
* **D7 (computational identifiability).** Let 𝒟_R be interactive distinguishers with oracle access to the interface and resources R. Advantage Adv_R(M, M′) = max_{D∈𝒟_R} |Pr[D^M = 1] − Pr[D^{M′} = 1]|. M ≈_R M′ iff Adv_R is negligible (below a stated tolerance). **P5 (E):** ≈_I ⊆ ≈_R, and ≈_R → ≈_I-with-statistical-closeness as R → ∞. K: this is computational indistinguishability and, for interactive access, pseudorandom function security (Goldreich–Goldwasser–Micali, as presented in Aaronson 2011 §7.1).
* **D8 (predictive equivalence).** Equality of predictions on a *test* policy class Π_test: M ≈_{Π_test} M′. Predictive equivalence for the observer's internal model Q means Q ≈_{Π_test} M.
* **P6 (E): why predictive ≠ structural.** A *structural* claim is a claim about a representation-level predicate ψ(M) (which operator, which edges, which latent variables). ψ is I-identifiable only if invariant on ≈_I classes (P3). For almost every natural ψ it is not: it is *not continuous* in any prediction metric (a model whose prediction error is ≤ ε can have arbitrarily different support, graph or operator set). Hence "predictively correct on Π_test" implies neither structural nor interventional correctness outside Π_test. K (as a phenomenon): non-identifiability and model misspecification generally; sparse-regression support instability; Locatello et al. (2019) Theorem 1 for latent coordinates. The programme's measurement (OOL: 0.42–0.62 predictive-not-structural) is an instance, not a discovery.

## 5. Four things that must not be conflated
1. **Implementation Σ**: what physically/computationally realises the dynamics (hardware, program text, source of randomness, scheduling, resource use).
2. **Effective laws** [M]_I (or its coarse-grained version [M]_{I,ε} at resolution ε): the equivalence class of interface-relative behaviour. The only layer an observer with interface I can identify even in principle.
3. **Mathematical representation** of the effective laws: equations, programs, causal graphs; many-to-one with 2 (non-unique), and with structural claims that may not be identifiable (P6).
4. **The observer's internal model** Q: a representation fitted under R; it may equal a representation of 2, a coarser or finer object, or something unrelated.
Maps: Σ ↦ M(Σ) is many-to-one (different implementations realise the same behaviour); M ↦ [M]_I is many-to-one; [M]_I ↦ representation is one-to-many. **P7 (E):** an implementation feature f(Σ) is identifiable for an observer with interface I and resources R only if it factors through [M]_I (unbounded R) or through [M]_R-equivalence (bounded R). Features that do not factor (e.g. the physical substrate; the choice of one of two bisimilar programs) are unidentifiable *by construction*. "Substrate independence" in Bostrom's argument is the corresponding assumption that the substrate does not factor through behaviour of the simulated minds.

## 6. A five-way classification (standard; each case has known archetypes)
| class | definition | archetypes and sources |
|---|---|---|
| 1 fundamentally unidentifiable | ∃ M′ ≈_I M with the property differing | unreachable transitions (P2); behaviourally equivalent realisations (state-space similarity; bisimilar twins); hidden-regime vs random-time change in one trajectory (phase 9 Prop F); threshold above actuator reach (S1) |
| 2 identifiable, computationally intractable | [M]_I identifiable information-theoretically but no R-bounded procedure finds it | key recovery of a pseudorandom function family with query access: a seed of n bits is determined by O(n) queries yet finding it breaks the PRF (Aaronson 2011 §7.1, GGM); learning regular/context-free languages from examples is as hard as breaking RSA (Kearns–Valiant, as reported there); sparse-PCA detection has a computational gap conditional on planted clique (Berthet–Rigollet 2013) |
| 3 identifiable and tractable | identifiable and learnable in poly(R) | generic identifiability of HMMs from 2k+1 consecutive observations when C(k+κ−1, κ−1) ≥ r (Allman–Matias–Rhodes 2009, Thm 6; Kruskal) with spectral learning under rank conditions (Hsu–Kakade–Zhang, not accessed); linear PSRs of size = rank of the system-dynamics matrix (Singh et al. 2004) |
| 4 identifiable only after particular interventions | ≈_{Π₀}-class bigger than ≈_I | interventional Markov equivalence (Hauser–Bühlmann 2012); the Causal Hierarchy Theorem; the programme's "revealing action" barrier (phase 7, X1 H2: with a policy that never uses the revealing action, rejection stayed at the type-I level, e.g. 6/600 for truth t05) |
| 5 identifiable only under extra structural assumptions | 𝓜 must be restricted | unsupervised disentanglement impossible without inductive biases (Locatello et al. 2019, Thm 1); causal representations identifiable up to ∼CRL from interventions (von Kügelgen et al. 2023, Thms 3.2, 3.4); Kruskal rank conditions; Takens/embedding genericity (not accessed); ergodicity/mixing for single-trajectory consistency (not accessed) |
**Is this standard?** Each row is standard in its own community (system identification, learning theory, cryptography, causal inference). I did not find, in what I accessed, a treatment that puts them on one axis system *for embedded observers with controlled interfaces*; whether one exists in formal learning theory or philosophy of science (Kelly; Kelly–Schulte; Wolpert) remains **unverified**.

## 7. What "embedded" adds: a candidate formalisation (not a result)
The lab observer of the programme's experiments has ρ = episodic with reset and Π_I = all admissible programmes. An embedded observer has (a) **ρ = single trajectory**: no i.i.d. episodes, so identification needs recurrence/ergodicity of the system (or extra premises); laws and initial/regime conditions are confounded unless a mixing premise holds (phase 9 Prop F; the cosmological analogue is cosmic variance); (b) **reach restriction 𝒞**: the reachable set R_I is a strict subset of histories; effective laws outside it are unidentifiable by P2 (the philosophical analogue is observationally indistinguishable spacetimes, Malament/Glymour/Manchak — not accessed; SEP underdetermination §3.2 discusses Earman's spacetime examples); (c) **R_obs bounded by the host's R**: the observer's computation is part of the system's computation; (d) **self-inclusion**: actions of the observer are part of the state; Wolpert (2008) proves, independent of physical laws, that no two *distinguishable* inference devices can weakly infer each other (Thm 1) and no two devices can strongly infer each other (Thm 3). **C (open, stated as a question only):** for controlled hidden-state classes, is there a strict hierarchy single-trajectory-passive ⊂ single-trajectory-interventional ⊂ episodic ⊂ counterfactual-replay in terms of identification in the limit, and what structural premises (recurrence, analyticity, finite memory) close the gaps? Probably largely derivable from Gold/Angluin/Kelly, ergodic theory and the causal hierarchy; this must be settled by the literature gate (`research-direction-decision.md`).

## 8. Role of learned world models
* **They cannot change identifiability.** Identifiability is a property of (𝓜, I, success notion); a learner is a procedure within it. Information-theoretic limits (P2, P6, Le Cam) bind every learner.
* **They affect tractability and approximation.** A learned latent model is an R-bounded representation; its quality is a statistical–computational–approximation question (class 2/3).
* **Representations can discard identifiable information.** A latent state trained to predict under Π_train is predictively sufficient only for histories in R_{Π_train} (P2); outside it nothing constrains the model (coverage). Disentanglement-type structure is not identifiable from observation alone (Locatello Thm 1) and with interventions only up to component-wise reparametrisation and permutation under assumptions (von Kügelgen et al.). Causal-abstraction theory (Beckers–Halpern 2019; they argue the earlier exact-transformation notion is too broad and propose constructive abstraction) is the correct language for "does Q preserve the interventional content of M?"
* **Distinguishing observationally equivalent mechanisms:** impossible for any predictive model if the equivalence is I-wide (P2/P3); possible for a model trained *with* the discriminating interventions (class 4) — a statement about data, not architecture.
* **Active interventions can overcome representation limits but not equivalence;** the programme's experiments found no benefit from learned intervention selection over fixed sweeps; theory predicts none unless the model class is misspecified in a way the design score can exploit (Le Cam score; EIG pathologies under misspecification, Rainforth et al.).
* **Classical methods suffice for the identifiability questions** (realization theory, spectral PSR/HMM methods, likelihood tests). World models matter, if at all, as bounded-resource instances in high-dimensional nonparametric regimes — **not essential to the strongest research question**. Failures of a particular GRU/RSSM (programme experiments) are architecture-and-coverage limitations, not fundamental epistemic limits.

## 9. Relation to Bostrom's argument
* **Bostrom (2003)** argues a trilemma: (1) f_P ≈ 0 (almost no civilisations reach a posthuman stage), (2) f_I ≈ 0 (almost no posthuman civilisation runs ancestor-simulations), (3) f_sim ≈ 1 (we are almost certainly simulated), from f_sim = f_P f_I N_I / (f_P f_I N_I + 1) and a "bland indifference principle" over which observer one is. It rests on *substrate independence* and computational-power estimates. It is a **credence** argument, not an identifiability claim; Bostrom himself writes that, supposing (3), the best guide to how the creators set up the world "is the standard empirical study of the universe we see" and that the chief empirical importance of (3) lies in its role in the tripartite conclusion. (Quoted/paraphrased from the extracted text.)
* **Chalmers** (The Matrix as Metaphysics; accessed via fetch, processed text): the Matrix Hypothesis is a *metaphysical* hypothesis (computational + creation + mind-body components), not a skeptical one; "from the inside, there is no way to tell for sure" — no empirical test is proposed.
* **Equivalence.** If the simulation is faithful (same interface behaviour), then simulated and non-simulated realities are ≈_I-equivalent *by definition*; no observer with interface I can detect it (P2, P3). Detection requires non-equivalence, i.e. artifacts that enter behaviour.
* **Artifacts are not necessary.** Whether a simulation has detectable artifacts depends on the simulator's design choices relative to its resources, not on the concept of simulation. Beane, Davoudi & Savage (2014) derive bounds only for an explicitly *assumed* class: a classical hypercubic lattice with unimproved Wilson fermions ("pedestrian approach"); they obtain b⁻¹ ≳ 10¹¹ GeV from the cosmic-ray cutoff for that class. Their result rules out (at that level) that class, and is silent on improved or finer discretisations. This is precisely interface-relative equivalence: the test depends on arbitrary assumptions about the simulator.
* **Can a simulated observer identify host properties?** Only those that factor through its interface behaviour (P7): effective laws, resolution limits that enter behaviour, computational-complexity side effects visible to bounded distinguishers. Not the substrate; not which of two bisimilar programs runs; not the host clock unless it leaks through the interface.
* **Finite computation.** A budget-limited host may have to approximate; approximation error is observable only if it exceeds noise and if the observer can design the discriminating intervention (class 4 + statistical identifiability). Pseudorandomness is a special case: a PRG-driven world is ≈_R-equivalent to a truly random one for bounded observers and ≈_I-distinguishable for unbounded ones (P5, class 2). Whether an embedded observer's resources can exceed the host's is a separate question (trivially no for a faithful emulation of the observer; Wolpert's results are more general).
* **Distinctions to keep:** logical possibility (Chalmers: coherent), mathematical identifiability (this framework), physical testability (conditional on simulator design, Beane et al.), empirical evidence (none from the toy experiments), Bayesian priors (Kipping 2020 applies Bayesian model comparison and obtains near-even posterior odds under his assumptions — search-summary level only, not read). **No probability of simulation is inferred from any programme experiment.**
* **Assessment:** the Bostrom connection is primarily philosophical. The formal connection is a negative one: the simulation hypothesis, in the form where the simulation is behaviourally faithful, is *not* an empirical hypothesis relative to the interface; empirical content arises only from added assumptions about the simulator (which are the assumptions Beane et al. state, and which Bostrom's own argument does not supply).
