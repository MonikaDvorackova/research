## Philosophical analysis

Formal statements refer to [formal-framework.md](formal-framework.md). Sources for the philosophical background (Duhem–Quine underdetermination, Bayesian confirmation, observer selection) are **not yet verified or cited**; the argument below is stated so that it does not depend on any particular reading of them.

### 1. Bostrom's argument, as relevant here

Bostrom (2003) argues that at least one of three propositions holds: almost all civilisations at our stage go extinct before becoming posthuman; almost no posthuman civilisations run significant numbers of ancestor simulations; we are almost certainly living in a simulation. The argument assumes substrate-independence of consciousness and that simulated observers' evidence is indistinguishable from that of non-simulated ones, which motivates an indifference step across observers with the same evidence. (Argument text not re-read this session; verify against the paper before submission.)

The experiments in this project **do not test the trilemma**. They concern a narrower question: what can an agent that only acts and observes conclude about how its environment is generated.

### 2. Three inferences

| Level | Claim | Formal object |
|---|---|---|
| L1 Discrepancy | The environment's interventional law differs from that of a reference (E0, or the agent's theory Q) | Test of H0 against H1 (Prop. 2, 5, 6) |
| L2 Mechanism | The environment is produced by a mechanism of a given class (e.g. a learned generative model) | Posterior over mechanisms (Prop. 1, 4) |
| L3 Simulation | The environment is implemented as a computation by agents or systems outside the inhabitants' ontology | Predicate S on mechanisms (Prop. 4) |

### 3. When each inference is valid or invalid

**L1 is valid if** (A1) the agent can repeat or otherwise structure interventions so that a test with known size exists; (A2) the null model it tests against is correct for the reference, or its misfit is accounted for (Prop. 6). **It is invalid, or reduces to model misfit, when** the null is the agent's own estimated theory and the interventions go beyond its experience: false alarms are bounded only by α + TV(E0, Q), and TV can be large (Prop. 3(b)). The experiments show this effect for out-of-distribution interventions.

**L2 is valid only if** (B1) the mechanism class has an observable signature that differs from all rivals (κ(C) ∩ κ(M∖C) = ∅), and (B2) the agent can resolve that difference with the interventions available (power; Prop. 2, 5). B1 usually fails: many mechanisms share a kernel (Cor. 1). Even a confirmed L1 shows that the kernel differs from a reference; it does not show that the difference was produced by a *learned model* rather than by any other departure. The matched-divergence control `Ealt` in the experiment tests exactly this: a real parameter shift with the same divergence from E0 is flagged just as readily as the learned model.

**L3 is valid only if** (C1) the artifact premise: simulation of the relevant kind leaves signatures in the kernel that the reference class lacks; (C2) the reference premise: the comparison class is itself non-simulated (false for any reference environment inside a laboratory or a model); (C3) the bridge premise from "learned computational model" to Bostrom's "ancestor simulation containing conscious observers", which requires substrate-independence and the other assumptions of the argument. None of C1–C3 is established by observation (Prop. 4).

### 4. Theses

**T1 (fidelity-indexed testability).** The unrestricted claim "the world is a simulation" is insulated from observational test: by Prop. 4 data affect it only through the artifact premise, and by Prop. 5 a faithful simulator yields near-zero evidential force from any test. What can be tested are *fidelity-indexed* sub-hypotheses, of the form "the world is generated by a simulator of class C with resolution r, under interventions of class I". Such a sub-hypothesis can be **refuted** by a calibrated detection, and only that.

**T2 (asymmetry of detection).** A detection licenses at most a refutation of the specific hypothesis it was designed against (or an admission of misfit of the reference); a non-detection confirms nothing about S in proportion to fidelity (Prop. 5). Hence "no anomaly found" is not evidence of base reality, and "anomaly found" is not evidence of simulation without the artifact and reference premises.

**T3 (relation to Bostrom).** Bostrom's argument builds in high-fidelity simulation. In the present framework high fidelity means small τ, so the argument is empirically insulated by design from the detection programme. This does not weaken the argument; it shows that its three disjuncts cannot be adjudicated by experiments of the kind studied here, and that proposals such as lattice-cutoff tests (Beane et al.) test fidelity-indexed sub-hypotheses, not the trilemma.

T1–T3 are philosophical theses supported by the formal propositions; they are not empirical findings of the experiment. Whether T1–T3 are already stated in the philosophical literature (Birch 2013, Chalmers 2022, and others) must be checked; this has been done at abstract level only.

### 5. Limits

- The formal analysis assumes the agent can interact repeatedly and that a reference law exists. A real observer has neither with certainty.
- Learned world models are not conscious observers; no inference about simulated consciousness is drawn.
- No generalisation from toy environments to the physical universe is made without bridge premises C1–C3.


## Reassessment after the literature review and the ablations (Phase 3)

### What is anticipated in the literature

Having read Chalmers (2024, both papers) in full and the other sources at abstract level (`literature/philosophical-review.md`):

- **T1 and T2 in qualitative form are largely known.** Perfect simulations are undetectable and we cannot know we are not in one (Chalmers's explicit epistemic thesis); lower-grade or imperfect simulations are detectable and may be sceptical scenarios (Chalmers, citing Schneider and Schwitzgebel); physical detection proposals target particular imperfections (Beane et al., Campbell et al.). The point that empirical equivalence is relative to auxiliary assumptions is Laudan and Leplin's.
- **T3 is a corollary** of the perfect-simulation case: Bostrom builds in simulations good enough for conscious observers, so detection-style evidence cannot bear on the trilemma.
- **Proposition 4 overlaps in spirit** with Chalmers's defeat argument by indifference: once most beings with matching experiences are sims, experience-internal or external-perceptual evidence does not shift credence.

None of these should be presented as new.

### What the experiments add to the three-way distinction

| Inference | What the experiments show | What they cannot show |
|---|---|---|
| **L1 Detectability of a discrepancy** | Detectability is a property of the *pair* (environment, observer's theory), not of the environment alone. The same environment, interventions and test give FPR about 0.05 and perfect detection (exact theory B; covered theory C), FPR up to 1.000 and inverted detection (neural theories trained only in-distribution: baseline and A1), or high AUROC with unusable calibration (different model class, A2). The information to discriminate exists throughout (oracle AUROC 1.000 under OOD interventions). | Anything about the physical world. |
| **L2 Identification of a generative mechanism** | The score tracks the size of the divergence in the probed region. With a well-specified theory, W ranks above the matched real mismatch AltOOD under adaptive probing, because the probe concentrates where W's error is largest. That ranking cannot be read as identifying a learned model: matching controls on one divergence summary does not match them under another probe distribution. | Whether a mechanism *class* can be identified: Corollary 1 says kernel-equal mechanisms cannot be separated, and the experiment has no kernel-equal pair other than E0/E0split (which is not separated, as predicted). |
| **L3 Justified inference of simulation** | Nothing. No experiment bears on the artifact, reference or bridge premises. The experiment's own "reference" is a laboratory object whose status as non-simulated is stipulated. | Everything about C1-C3. |

### Revised theses

**T4 (theory-relativity of detection; new candidate, supported by the ablations).** Whether an anomaly can be found, and what a found anomaly shows, depends on the observer's own theory and on how far that theory's evidential base covers the interventions performed. A learned theory trained only on a narrow range of interventions produces false alarms against the true environment and can rate a faithful-in-range imitation as *more* consistent with the theory than reality is. Detection claims therefore license at most "the world departs from my theory under these interventions" (L1); a departure under interventions outside the theory's coverage is weak evidence about the world. This is the Duhem-Quine point (a test confronts a theory together with its auxiliaries) in quantitative form for learned observers; the qualitative point is old, and I do not claim it as new. What may be new is the quantified instance (Propositions 3(b) and 6, and conditions A1, A2, B, C).

**T5 (coverage bound on fidelity-indexed testability; interpretive).** Combining T1 with T4: a fidelity-indexed simulation hypothesis can be tested only in regimes the observer's theory already covers or can be derived to cover. Hypotheses about regimes the observer has never visited (for instance extreme energies) lie outside this range, because the observer has no reference for them (premise C2). This is an interpretation of the experimental structure, not an empirical result about physics.

**Status of T1-T3.** Retained as clarifications of known points, positioned as such. They are not the contribution.

### The smallest defensible reformulation

Make the paper about T4/T5: *what an anomaly shows when the observer's own theory is a learned model*, with the three-way distinction and Propositions 4-6 as the supporting framework, the ablation table as the evidence, and Bostrom's argument as the motivating case (not as the target of the experiments). The claims are then: (i) a precise statement of what L1/L2/L3 each require; (ii) a bound-based explanation of why detection claims are theory-relative; (iii) a controlled demonstration, with ablations and an exact-theory upper bound, that the relativity is large and depends on coverage. The novelty is the quantified, learned-observer instance of a known philosophical point. Whether that is enough for *Minds and Machines* is doubtful; see the reviewer assessment.

## Phase 4 reassessment: the inference ladder, Bostrom's proviso, and what remains defensible

Evidence base: Bostrom (2003) and Chalmers (2024) read in full; formal learning theory, Chalmers's "Matrix as Metaphysics", and several statistics and ML sources at abstract or summary level; the experiments and propositions of this repository. Brogioli, Norton, Laudan–Leplin (primary), Weatherson and Edge remain unread, so every "not found in the literature" below is provisional.

### Three kinds of claim, kept apart

- **Epistemic possibility**: "for all the observer can tell from observation, hypothesis H is open". Chalmers's thesis that we cannot know we are not in a simulation is of this kind; Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 are its formal counterpart for perfect (kernel-equal) simulations.
- **Empirical evidence**: observations that change the rational credence in H. Here the apparatus is likelihood ratios bounded by the total-variation distance (Propositions 5 and 7(a)), relative to a stated theory and intervention class.
- **Metaphysical conclusion**: "the world is (or is not) a simulation". Nothing in the propositions or the experiments entails it, in either direction. The experiments concern learned dynamics models in a toy environment; they establish no fact about the physical universe, and no step to the physical universe is made without the bridge premises below.

### The inference ladder

| 2. ### Connection to Bostrom's argument, stated narrowly

1. Bostrom's argument is not about detection; it is a conditional probabilistic argument whose credence step is licensed by an indifference principle **conditional on the absence of discriminating information**. This project concerns that proviso and nothing else.
2. Bostrom's own text builds in the property that makes the proviso hold: simulators can edit anomalies noticed by simulated minds or rerun, so that the simulation is "indistinguishable from physical reality for human minds". In our terms the simulator drives the divergence along the observer's interventions to zero.
3. Under that stipulation, the formal results say: a null result carries no evidence against the simulation hypothesis (Prop. 5: likelihood ratio within 1 ± τ/(1 − α)), and no sequence of such results overturns the indifference prior unless n ≈ ln ω / KL, which is unbounded as KL → 0 (Corollary). So **the three disjuncts are empirically insulated from detection-style evidence by the argument's own assumptions**; the experiments neither support nor weaken them.
4. What the study adds is on the observer's side: even where simulators are imperfect and KL is large, the observer's *own theory* decides whether the imperfection is noticed, and a learned theory can rate a faithful-in-range imitation as more normal than reality (H1, H7). A detection (or non-detection) reported by a fallible observer is therefore weak evidence about the generating mechanism until the theory's coverage of the probed regime is established.
5. The experiments do **not** show that the physical universe is simulated, that it is not, or that a particular detection programme (for example lattice-cutoff tests) would succeed or fail; the toy dynamics have no bearing on physics beyond illustrating the structure of the inference.

### Which claims remain defensible after the literature review

| Claim | Defensible? | Basis and caveat |
|---|---|---|
| T1: the unrestricted simulation hypothesis is not testable; fidelity-indexed hypotheses are | Yes, **as a clarification, not as a contribution** | Established by Bostrom's built-in indistinguishability, Chalmers, and formal learning theory (indistinguishable hypotheses cannot be identified). |
| T2: detection can refute specific hypotheses; non-detection confirms nothing in proportion to fidelity | Yes, as a clarification | Popper/Duhem tradition; the quantitative form (Prop. 5) is an elementary bound. |
| T3: Bostrom's argument is insulated from detection-style evidence | Yes, as a corollary | Follows from his own anomaly-editing assumption; Chalmers's perfect-simulation case. |
| T4: anomaly detection is relative to the observer's theory and its coverage | Yes, with an experimentally grounded quantified instance | Qualitatively the Duhem–Quine point; the inversion for learned likelihoods is known for static models (Nalisnick et al.; Ren et al.); the interventional, sequential instance and its robustness across architectures and samples (H1) are not found in the literature I reached. |
| T6: learning from the environment under test cannot by itself supply the reference needed for detection (adaptation absorbs the anomaly) | Yes, for the protocol tested, with a formal explanation (Prop. 8(c)) | Posterior consistency is textbook statistics; the experiment shows absorption for batch fine-tuning only; a Bayesian model-selection observer with a suitable class would behave differently (Prop. 8(a)) and was not tested. |
| T7: the evidence needed to defeat the indifference proviso is about ln ω / KL | Yes, as an elementary quantification | Possibly new as an application to Bostrom's proviso; mathematically elementary. |
| T5: testability of fidelity-indexed hypotheses is bounded by the observer's coverage of the probed regime | Plausible interpretation, **not established** | Supported by the coverage conditions (C, B) in a toy; no support for physical regimes. |

### The strongest defensible contribution, and a more precise question

The strongest contribution is a **quantified, experimentally grounded account of why an observer's detection claims are relative to its own theory, and why updating that theory on the environment under test cannot repair this**, applied to the evidential proviso of Bostrom's indifference principle. It is integrative: philosophy-of-science points (Duhem–Quine; underdetermination) made precise with elementary statistics and illustrated with learned models. No genuinely new theorem is claimed.

- **Revised research question.** *What can an observer with a fallible, learned theory of its world legitimately infer from anomalies under its own interventions, and how much discriminating evidence would be required to defeat the no-discriminating-information proviso of Bostrom's indifference principle?*
- **Revised title.** *What Does an Anomaly Show? Theory-Relative Limits on Detecting Simulated Environments from Within.*
- **Subtitle for the original framing, if retained.** "Epistemic identifiability of embedded agents" is acceptable only if the paper explains that identifiability is relative to a hypothesis class and a reference regime (Props. 1, 4, 8).

### Where the limits of detection come from

From the environment (what the probed regime makes distinguishable: ceiling set by divergence under the chosen interventions); from the observer (how much of that ceiling its theory can realise, including inversion); and from the observer's knowledge of the alternative (composite versus simple). In the experiments the environment sets the ceiling, the observer's theory determines the realised fraction (0.026 to 0.989 AUROC at the same interventions), and in the in-distribution regime the environment itself is the binding limit. For the physical-universe question the analogous statement is a framework, not a finding.
