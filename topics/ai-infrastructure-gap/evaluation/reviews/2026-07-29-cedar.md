## 1. Design intent

Cedar is a language for **writing authorization policies** and **making authorization decisions** based on those policies. Applications preface protected operations with a call to Cedar’s authorization engine asking whether a request is authorized; the application then allows the operation or returns an error according to the decision (`docs.cedarpolicy.com` introduction).

Cedar is designed to **decouple business logic from authorization logic**. Policies describe who (principal) may perform which actions on which resources, optionally under what context. Entities (application data Cedar must see) and a request **context** (transient/session data) are supplied with the request. An optional **schema** declares entity types, actions, and context shapes so policies can be **validated** when created or updated; Cedar **does not use the schema when evaluating** an authorization request (introduction; `schema.html`; `validation.html`).

Authorization responses comprise an **Allow** or **Deny** decision plus **diagnostics** (determining policies and evaluation errors) (`terminology.html`; `authorization.html`).

**Intent for this review:** evaluate whether Cedar’s **policy language**, **authorization model**, **schema/entities/requests**, and **evaluation/diagnostics** preserve information needed for faithful reconstruction of legally relevant AI-supported decisions—not whether Cedar is a good authorization language in general.

## 2. Observed Cedar capabilities

(First-class / documented only.)

### 2.1 Policy language

- Policies are text documents with **effect** (`permit` / `forbid`), mandatory **scope** (principal, action, resource), optional **conditions** (`when` / `unless`), and optional **annotations** (`syntax-policy.html`).
- Annotations are arbitrary key-value metadata stored with the policy; they **have no impact on policy evaluation** and exist for other services/applications that process policies (`syntax-policy.html`).
- Static policies and **policy templates** / template-linked policies are documented (`terminology.html`).

### 2.2 Authorization model and requests (PARC)

- An authorization request asks: “Can this principal take this action on this resource in this context?” Formally **PARC**: principal, action, resource (entity references) and context (a record) (`authorization.html`; `terminology.html`).
- The application must gather relevant entity data (attributes, group/hierarchy membership) and context, and supply relevant policies (`terminology.html` Policy evaluation).

### 2.3 Entities and schema

- **Entities** have type + identifier (UID), attributes, and optional parent/hierarchy membership (`terminology.html`; `schema.html`).
- A **schema** declares entity types (membership and attributes), actions (applicability and context shape), namespaces, and optional common types; Cedar and JSON schema formats are interchangeable (`schema.html`).
- Schema supports **policy validation**, **entity validation**, and **request validation** as separate concerns from authorization evaluation (`validation.html`; `security.html`). Schema is optional but recommended.

### 2.4 Evaluation semantics and diagnostics

- Per-policy evaluation yields `true`, `false`, or `error` (`authorization.html`).
- Combination rules: **default deny**; **forbid overrides permit**; **skip on error** (erroneous policies do not factor into the decision; errors appear in diagnostics) (`authorization.html`).
- Authorization **response** includes the decision and **diagnostics**: determining policy IDs (reasons) and any evaluation errors (`authorization.html`; `terminology.html`).

### 2.5 Explicit non-capabilities (observation)

Primary sources do **not** define first-class Cedar concepts for: durable decision/audit logs; decision episode identifiers beyond determining policy IDs; policy version axes bound into the response; legal jurisdiction; AI model/prompt constructs; relied-upon vs available evidence distinctions; or external governance systems. Attribute contents of entities/context are application-defined.

## 3. Checklist interpretation

Nearest claim-inventory item: **C6** (OPA/Cedar-style decision logs necessary but not sufficient for full justification/validity reconstruction in AI-mediated decisions).

Under checklist v0.1.0, interpret Cedar as an **authorization policy language and authorizer**, not as a complete decision-knowledge preservation system.

| Distinction | Application to Cedar |
|---|---|
| **Policy representation vs policy provenance** | `permit`/`forbid` policies + conditions represent authorization rules; diagnostics return **determining policy IDs**. Annotations may carry metadata for external processors but do not affect evaluation and are not a systematic provenance model. No first-class policy-version field in the authorization response. |
| **Policy evaluation vs legal justification** | Evaluation yields Allow/Deny from PARC against policies—an **authorization decision**, not a legal justification record for an AI-supported business decision. |
| **Authorization decisions vs preservation of decision knowledge** | Cedar defines how decisions are **computed** and what diagnostics accompany the response; it does **not** define durable retention of PARC, entities, or policies as an audit log. Reconstruction of a historical episode requires application- or service-level recording outside the language SUT. |

## 5. Evaluation boundary

Only capabilities explicitly represented by Cedar itself receive credit:

- Policy language constructs, PARC authorization requests, entities, schema, evaluation semantics, and official authorization-response diagnostics may be credited **where documented**.
- Organization-specific entity attributes, application-specific metadata, custom audit logs, Amazon Verified Permissions, AWS integrations, and external governance systems receive **no** credit unless they are first-class Cedar concepts (they are not, under this SUT).
- Implementation practices (e.g. “we always log PARC and policy version”) do **not** influence scoring.
- Annotations are first-class *syntax* but explicitly non-evaluative metadata for external processors—they do **not** raise scores for provenance, legal context, or systematic reconstruction categories.

## 6. Threats to validity

- Spec/docs evaluation: real deployments may retain richer audit trails via non-Cedar components; those must not inflate Cedar scores.
- Schema is optional and unused at evaluation time; invalid or incomplete entity slices can change outcomes; level-based slicing is an application responsibility (`authorization.html`; level-validation docs exist but slicing implementation is not provided by Cedar).
- Skip-on-error means erroneous policies are omitted from the decision while appearing in diagnostics—reconstruction must interpret diagnostics carefully.
- Policy templates change linked policies dynamically when the template changes (`terminology.html`)—historical reconstruction requires external versioning of templates/policies.
- Language version (4.5) ≠ SDK crate version; citing the wrong versioning axis can mis-pin the SUT.

---

## 7. Remaining uncertainty

- Whether formal Lean models in the broader cedar-spec ecosystem encode additional reconstructable structure beyond the language guide—out of scope unless separately version-pinned and reviewed.
- How often determining policy IDs plus externally stored policy text suffice for IC-08 in practice—empirical/composition question.
- Claim **C6** receives unit-level support here (authorization evaluation/diagnostics strong; full AI justification/validity not) but is **not** updated in aggregate claim inventories by this task.

---

## 8. Overall assessment

**Cedar Policy Language Version 4.5 systematically supports representation and evaluation of authorization policies** (permit/forbid, scope, conditions), structured **PARC** requests with **entities** and **context**, optional **schema** validation, and authorization **responses** that include Allow/Deny plus **diagnostics** (determining policies and errors). That is strong for *computing* authorization decisions and for identifying which policies determined a response.

Cedar does **not**, by itself, systematically preserve the full information required for faithful reconstruction of **legally relevant AI-supported decisions**: it defines no durable decision log, no decision-episode identity, no policy-version binding in the response, and no first-class reliance/legal-justification model. Distinguishing **authorization evaluation** from **legal justification** and **authorization decisions** from **complete decision-knowledge preservation** is essential.

This aligns with treating Cedar as unit-level evidence for claim-inventory **C6** (authorization machinery necessary for authorization reconstruction; not sufficient alone for the full AI decision triad)—without promoting aggregate claim status in this task.
