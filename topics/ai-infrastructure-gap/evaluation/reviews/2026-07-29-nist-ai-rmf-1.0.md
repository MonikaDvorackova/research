## 1. Design intent

AI RMF 1.0 is a **voluntary** framework to improve organizations’ ability to incorporate **trustworthiness** considerations into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI products, services, and systems, and to better manage risks to individuals, organizations, and society (Front matter; Part 1).

An **AI system** is defined (adapted from OECD/ISO) as an engineered or machine-based system that can, for given objectives, generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations, or **decisions** influencing real or virtual environments, with varying autonomy (Part 1).

Part 2 **Core** comprises four functions—**GOVERN**, **MAP**, **MEASURE**, **MANAGE**—broken into categories and subcategories of outcomes and actions. Actions are **not** a checklist or ordered mandatory steps; users may select among subcategories based on resources and needs (§5). **GOVERN** is cross-cutting; MAP/MEASURE/MANAGE apply in system-specific lifecycle contexts.

**Profiles** tailor the Core to sector, use-case, or temporal (Current vs Target) contexts (§6).

The **Playbook** is an online companion of suggested tactical actions to achieve Core outcomes; it is voluntary and organizations may borrow as many or as few suggestions as apply (Framework §5; Playbook about text).

**Intent for this review:** evaluate whether AI RMF 1.0’s **explicit Core/Profiles concepts** preserve information needed for faithful reconstruction of historically situated, legally relevant AI-supported *decisions*—not whether the Framework is good risk management guidance in general.

## 2. Observed capabilities

(First-class / documented in AI RMF 1.0 unless noted as Playbook clarification only.)

### 2.1 Trustworthiness framing (Part 1)

Characteristics include valid/reliable, safe, secure/resilient, **accountable and transparent**, explainable/interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed (§3).

**Accountable and Transparent (§3.4):** Accountability presupposes transparency. Transparency reflects the extent to which information about an AI system and its outputs is available; meaningful transparency is role-/lifecycle-tailored. Scope is described as spanning design decisions and training data through model training and **whether and how post-deployment or end-user decisions were made and by whom**. Transparency answers “what happened”; explainability “how”; interpretability “why.” Maintaining **provenance of training data** and attribution of the AI system’s decisions to subsets of training data “can assist” transparency and accountability. Explainable systems “lend themselves to more thorough documentation, audit, and governance.”

These are **characteristic descriptions**, not Core subcategory mandates to retain every decision episode.

### 2.2 Core functions (Tables 1–4) — documentation-relevant outcomes (non-exhaustive)

| Function | Structurally relevant outcomes |
|---|---|
| **GOVERN** | Legal/regulatory AI requirements understood, managed, **documented** (1.1); risk-management process/outcomes established via transparent policies (1.4); ongoing **monitoring** and periodic review (1.5); AI system **inventory** mechanisms (1.6); roles/responsibilities **documented** (2.1); leadership responsibility for risk decisions (2.3); teams **document** risks/impacts (4.2); testing, **incident** identification, information sharing (4.3); third-party contingency for failures/incidents (6.2). Documentation said to enhance transparency, human review, and accountability (§5.1 prose). |
| **MAP** | Intended purposes/context/risk tolerances/**system requirements** documented (1.x); tasks/methods and **knowledge limits** documented to assist AI actors’ decisions (2.1–2.2); TEVV considerations documented (2.3); benefits/costs/scope/operator proficiency/**human oversight** defined, assessed, documented (3.x); technology/legal risk mapping and internal controls documented (4.x); impact likelihood/magnitude using past uses and public incident reports documented (5.1). |
| **MEASURE** | Metrics/approaches documented (1.x); TEVV test sets/metrics/tools documented (2.1); performance/assurance criteria documented (2.3); functionality/behavior **monitored in production** (2.4); deployment beyond development conditions documented (2.5); safety/security/transparency-accountability/explainability/privacy/fairness/environmental aspects evaluated and documented (2.6–2.12); risk tracking and end-user feedback (3.x); measurement results documented (4.x). Measurement provides a **traceable basis** to inform management decisions where trustworthiness tradeoffs arise (§5.3 prose). |
| **MANAGE** | Risk treatments prioritized/documented; residual risks documented (1.x); strategies documented; respond/recover from unknown risks; supersede/disengage/deactivate (2.x); third-party monitoring documented (3.x); post-deployment monitoring plans including user input, **appeal and override**, decommissioning, **incident response**, recovery, **change management** (4.1). Systematic documentation practices from GOVERN used in MAP/MEASURE bolster transparency/accountability (§5.4 prose). |

### 2.3 Profiles (§6)

Profiles apply/tailor Core outcomes to context (e.g., sectoral examples). **Temporal profiles:** Current Profile (how AI risks are managed now) vs Target Profile (desired outcomes); comparing them reveals gaps. Profiles are **organizational risk-management state descriptions**, not decision-episode timelines.

### 2.4 Explicit non-capabilities (observation)

AI RMF 1.0 does **not** define: decision-episode identifiers; schemas for evidence relied upon in a concrete AI-supported outcome; authorization allow/deny records; binding of policy/model versions to individual decisions; or mandatory machine-readable decision logs. “Documentation” in Core overwhelmingly means documenting **risks, policies, TEVV, contexts, and monitoring**—not reconstructing each historical decision.

## 3. Checklist interpretation

Nearest claim-inventory item: **C1** (loosely coupled artifact retention insufficient for triad reconstruction).

| Distinction | Application to AI RMF 1.0 |
|---|---|
| **Governance of AI systems** | GOVERN policies, roles, inventory, risk culture. |
| **Documentation expectations** | Repeated “documented” outcomes for requirements, risks, TEVV, human oversight, treatments. |
| **Organizational processes** | MAP/MEASURE/MANAGE lifecycle risk activities; Profiles; monitoring and incident response. |
| **Preservation of individual decision episodes** | Not a Core outcome. Transparency *characteristic* mentions end-user decisions “by whom” as a scope ideal—not a required reconstructable record type. |
| **Evidence actually relied upon** | Objective evidence for *validation*; incident evidence for *risk documentation*; training-data provenance as optional assistance—not IC-11 for a decision. |
| **Legal or policy justification of an outcome** | Legal/regulatory requirements managed/documented at org level (GOVERN 1.1)—not justification of a specific decision under then-applicable rules. |
| **Temporal reconstruction** | Ongoing monitoring, change management, Current/Target Profiles—organizational over-time risk posture, not decision-time criteria binding. |

Playbook may illustrate *how* an organization might document models or set audit cadence; under the evaluation boundary those suggestions **do not** expand Framework first-class capabilities for scoring.

## 5. Evaluation boundary

Only concepts explicitly defined by AI RMF 1.0 receive credit:

- Core categories/subcategories and Profiles may be credited where they systematically structure outcomes.
- Recommendations receive credit only where **systematically structured** as Framework outcomes—not where merely aspirational trustworthiness prose without Core force.
- Generic governance recommendations must **not** be interpreted as first-class support for decision identity, decision evidence, authorization, legal justification, or temporal reconstruction of decisions.
- The Playbook may clarify Framework intent but must **not** introduce new first-class capabilities for scoring.
- Implementation examples, organizational practices, Generative AI Profile, and vendor tools receive **no** credit.

---

## 6. Threats to validity

- Framework is voluntary; users may select subsets of subcategories—scores reflect expressiveness of defined outcomes, not guaranteed adoption.
- “Documented” repeatedly means risk/TEVV/policy documentation; conflating that with decision-episode logs would inflate scores.
- §3.4 transparency language about end-user decisions is easy to over-read as a retention requirement; Core tables do not require episode IDs.
- Playbook evolution is frequent and separate from 1.0 version pin—using Playbook as capability source would break boundary.
- Later NIST profiles/extensions are explicitly out of SUT.

---

## 7. Remaining uncertainty

- Whether a *composition* of AI RMF organizational documentation + operational decision logs could satisfy the triad is a separate evaluation.
- How “temporal Profiles” interact with system versioning in practice is empirical/implementation.
- Claim **C1** receives unit-level illustration only; aggregate status is **not** updated.

---

## 8. Overall assessment

**NIST AI RMF 1.0 systematically structures organizational AI risk management** across Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage—including documentation of policies, contexts, human oversight, TEVV, monitoring, incidents, and change management, plus Current/Target Profiles. That is strong for **governing and documenting AI systems as risk objects**.

Against the reconstruction checklist, AI RMF 1.0 does **not** systematically preserve individual decision episodes, evidence actually relied upon in a concrete decision, authorization/legal justification of outcomes, or decision-time temporal binding. Faithful reconstruction of justification, authorization, and validity for legally relevant AI-supported decisions **fails** for AI RMF 1.0 alone—consistent with unit-level illustration of **C1**.
